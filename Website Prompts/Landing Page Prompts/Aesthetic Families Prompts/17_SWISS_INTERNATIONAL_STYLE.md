## Design System: SWISS / INTERNATIONAL STYLE

This section defines the strict visual identity, design tokens, and implementation guidelines for the **SWISS / INTERNATIONAL STYLE** aesthetic. You must implement these rules exactly as specified without deviation.

### A. SYNTHESIZED DESIGN TOKENS
- **Aesthetic Style:** SWISS / INTERNATIONAL STYLE

### B. COMPREHENSIVE STYLE SPECIFICATIONS

Ranked Industries (Most to Least Suited)

* **Financial Technology (FinTech) Platforms** — the precision, neutrality, and systematic clarity of Swiss design are perfectly aligned with financial data presentation
* **News & Information Journalism** — the International Style was literally developed for newspaper and magazine information design
* **Government & Public Sector Communications** — neutral, accessible, and systematically clear for diverse populations
* **Transportation & Wayfinding Systems** — Swiss design created the visual language of transit signage (the London Underground, the New York subway system)
* **Medical Devices & Healthcare Technology** — precision and clarity in life-critical information contexts
* **Academic Publishing & Research Institutions** — the communication of complex knowledge with maximal clarity
* **Data Visualization Products** — the systematic grid and typographic clarity of Swiss design are native to information graphics
* **Architecture Firms (Rationalist)** — the design philosophy of rational construction and systematic organization
* **Premium Consumer Electronics (Clean Utility)** — brands whose entire proposition is refined, purposeful function
International Organizations & Multilateral Institutions — the UN, WHO, and similar bodies use the International Style because it transcends national visual conventions

The Deterministic Application Guide: Swiss / International Style

* **WHY SWISS DESIGN WORKS** — THE PSYCHOLOGICAL FOUNDATION
The Swiss International Style, developed in the 1950s and 1960s by designers including Josef Müller-Brockmann and Armin Hofmann, was built on a radical proposition: design should be objectively communicative, not subjectively expressive. The designer's personality should disappear entirely. What should remain is pure information, organized by mathematical principles, in the most legible possible form.
This works psychologically through transparency of intent. When a design has no personal expression in it — no decorative choice that serves the designer's ego, no aesthetic preference that serves anyone's pleasure — the viewer trusts that every decision was made in service of communication. The design becomes a perfectly clear window through which information passes, unobstructed.
* **For complex information** — financial data, scientific findings, news analysis, transit directions — this transparency is extraordinarily valuable. The viewer does not waste cognitive resources interpreting the design's personality; they use all available cognitive resources on the information itself.

### COLOR

#### The Palette Logic

Swiss design's color palette is the most constrained and purposeful of all aesthetics. Color is used only to serve information — never to decorate.
The Swiss Color System
Primary Ground:

* White: `#FFFFFF` — the primary background. Clean, neutral, transparent.
* Black: `#000000` or `#111111` — the primary foreground. Maximum contrast, maximum legibility.

One Accent Color (The Entire Expressive Budget)

Swiss design allows precisely one accent color, used with surgical restraint. This color serves two purposes: to indicate the brand identity and to mark the highest-priority elements.
Classic Swiss accent colors:

* Red: `#CC0000` or `#E00000` — the most historically accurate Swiss design accent. References the Swiss flag and the bold red accents of classic Swiss posters by Müller-Brockmann.
* Deep blue: `#003399` or `#0040BB` — used when red would be too aggressive for the content context (financial, medical)
Black as the accent: some Swiss designs use no accent color at all — pure black and white with systematic grey values for hierarchy

The Grey Scale as a Color System

Swiss design uses grey systematically as a hierarchy tool:

* `#F5F5F5` — very light grey backgrounds for secondary information panels
* `#E0E0E0` — light grey for rule lines and borders
* `#9E9E9E` — medium grey for secondary text
* `#616161` — dark grey for tertiary information
* `#212121` — near-black for primary text

Each grey level corresponds to a specific information hierarchy level. This is a formal system, not a casual choice.

#### What to Strictly Avoid in Color

* **More than one accent color** — every additional color violates the systematic clarity
* **Gradients** — they are non-systematic and therefore invalid in Swiss design
* **Warm tones in the grey scale (beige, cream)** — Swiss grey is perfectly neutral, not warm
* **Decorative color use** — color must serve hierarchy or identity, never decoration

### TYPOGRAPHY

The Core Philosophy

Swiss typography is the most rigorously systematic of all aesthetic families. It was the Swiss designers who established Helvetica (1957) as the defining neutral typeface — a font so deliberately devoid of personality that it communicates pure information without editorial interference.

#### Font Classification

The Helvetica Foundation

Helvetica or Helvetica Neue: the canonical Swiss typeface. Its near-perfect neutrality is the result of meticulous optical adjustment — every curve, every junction, every proportion was refined to produce a letterform that reads without friction. It is the typographic equivalent of a perfectly clear window.
Neue Haas Grotesk (the digital revival of the original Helvetica): slightly more refined than the widely-distributed digital Helvetica
Akzidenz-Grotesk: the predecessor to Helvetica, slightly more quirky and humanist — preferred by designers who find Helvetica "too perfect"
* **Arial: a Helvetica substitute** — acceptable but acknowledged as a compromise
Inter or Source Sans Pro: acceptable contemporary substitutes for screen applications where Helvetica is not licensed

Why Helvetica?

Helvetica's designer, Max Miedinger, and Eduard Hoffmann created it specifically to be neutral — to have no personality that would interfere with the information it was conveying. The optical adjustments that make Helvetica work are invisible precisely because they are doing their job correctly. A typeface that reads perfectly is a typeface you do not notice.
The Swiss Typographic System
One typeface. Multiple weights. Strict size relationships.

Headline level 1 (H1): 48px to 64px, Medium (500) or Bold (700)
Headline level 2 (H2): 32px to 40px, Regular (400) or Medium (500)
Headline level 3 (H3): 22px to 28px, Medium (500)
Body text: 16px to 18px, Regular (400), line-height 1.5 to 1.6
Caption: 12px to 13px, Regular (400) or Medium (500)
Label: 11px, Medium (500), uppercase, tracking 0.08em to 0.12em

Alignment: Flush Left, Ragged Right

This is the most important typographic rule in Swiss design: all body text is set flush left, ragged right. Never justified (where both margins are flush), never centered. The reasons:

Flush-left text produces consistent inter-word spacing — every space between words is identical. Justified text stretches some spaces and compresses others, creating irregular rivers of white space that interrupt reading.
Ragged right creates an organic right edge that guides the eye back to the left margin without the visual monotony of a perfectly straight right edge.
Centered text (for body) requires the eye to find a new starting point for each line — exhausting for long reading.

Number Treatment

Swiss design, particularly in financial and data contexts, uses tabular (monospaced) numerals — where every digit occupies exactly the same horizontal width. This ensures that columns of numbers align perfectly vertically. Proportional numerals (where "1" is narrower than "8") are not used in data tables.

### SPACING & LAYOUT

* **The Grid** — The Absolute Foundation
Swiss design invented the modern typographic grid as a design system. Josef Müller-Brockmann's book "Grid Systems in Graphic Design" (1961) is the foundational text. Every spacing decision in Swiss design is derived from a mathematically consistent grid.
The Swiss Grid Construction Method

Establish the base unit: the line-height of the body text. If body text is 16px with 1.5 line-height, the base unit is 24px (16 × 1.5).
All spacing is multiples of this base unit: 24px, 48px, 72px, 96px, 120px, 144px...
Column width is derived from the grid: if the base unit is 24px and the content area is 1200px, a standard Swiss grid might use 12 columns of 72px each with 24px gutters.
Every element snaps to the baseline grid: headlines, body text, images, and captions all align their baselines to the 24px grid. This creates a visual order across the entire page — even when column arrangements vary, the baseline alignment creates coherence.

Why this specific method?

Because the baseline grid ensures that text on the left column and text on the right column are always vertically aligned — even when they have different sizes and weights. This alignment is imperceptible to most readers, but its absence creates a subtle visual discomfort that readers attribute to low quality. Its presence creates a systematic harmony that readers attribute to precision.
Layout Structure

* **Swiss layout uses modules** — the grid cells — as the organizing unit of the entire composition. Content blocks span multiples of modules. Images span multiples of modules. Negative space spans multiples of modules. The grid is absolute.

### BORDERS, SHAPES & FORMS

Rule Lines as Active Design Elements
Swiss design uses rule lines (horizontal and vertical lines) more deliberately than any other aesthetic except Bauhaus. These are not decorative lines — they are structural elements that organize information:

A 1px black rule above a headline at the start of a new article
A 1px rule separating columns in a two-column layout
A 1px rule at the top of the footer
A 2px to 4px rule in the accent color marking the highest-priority information element

#### Border Radius

0px on all elements. Swiss design is geometric and constructed. Rounded corners are a concession to approachability that the International Style does not make.
Negative Space as a Compositional Tool

Swiss design uses negative space with the same deliberateness as positive elements. An empty module in the grid is as intentional as a filled one. This is why Swiss-influenced layouts sometimes have large white areas that seem "empty" — the emptiness is the design.
Form Elements

Inputs: a bottom-border-only underline at 1px black — no decorative border treatment
Active/focus state: the accent color appears as the bottom border at 2px
Labels: above the input, in the caption size and color
Submit: a flat rectangle with 0px radius, black background, white text, or the accent color — clean, systematic, no decoration

### UI COMPONENTS

#### Navigation

Swiss navigation is systematic and information-dense:

A thin 1px black rule running the full page width at the very top
Below this: the logo/brand name at body text size (not large, not small — appropriate) on the left; navigation links on the right
* **Below the navigation: another 1px black rule** — the navigation is bracketed by thin rules
Navigation links: in the body font at 14px to 15px, regular weight, with the active page indicated by the accent color (not by underline, not by bold — by color only)

Tables

* **Swiss design's native UI component** — the data table:

1px horizontal rules between rows only (no vertical rules)
Column header row: the base text color, Medium weight, uppercase with slight tracking — clearly differentiated from data rows
* Alternating row backgrounds: pure white alternating with `#F9F9F9` — barely perceptible, just enough for the eye to track rows
Cell padding: 10px to 12px vertical, 16px horizontal — comfortable without being generous
Numbers: tabular numerals, right-aligned within the cell — numbers read from right to left when comparing

### IMAGERY & TEXTURE

#### Photography

Swiss design treats photography as information-bearing, not decorative:

* **Documentary, journalistic photography** — the subject clearly visible, the composition clear and uncropped
Photography used at full width of the grid module it occupies — never arbitrary sizes
Never vignetting, filtering, or decorative treatment of photographs — the image is the information; decorating it obscures it
When photography is abstract (used for visual interest): it references the natural geometry of the subject — aerial photography, macro photography of regular natural patterns

Information Graphics

The Swiss style's primary "imagery" is information graphics — charts, diagrams, maps:

Line charts: thin black lines, gridlines at the base unit interval, axis labels in the caption style
Bar charts: flat black or single-accent-color bars, no gradients, no 3D
* **Maps: reduced to essential information** — roads, boundaries, key points. No decorative mapping elements.

Zero Texture

Swiss design surfaces are perfectly flat. No texture of any kind. The organization of information IS the aesthetic — no texture is needed or permitted.

### ANIMATION & MOTION

Swiss design's animation is purely functional:
Appropriate animations:

State changes only: 150ms, no easing curve preference — the change communicates the state, not a performance
Data chart animation: bars or lines draw themselves from left to right on page load — this is a functional animation that communicates "the data is loading"
* Table row highlight on hover: the row background changes from white to a very light grey (0 to `#F5F5F5`) over 100ms — communicating which row the user is examining
No page transitions beyond a simple instantaneous switch

What to strictly avoid:

Any animation that serves aesthetic rather than informational purposes
* **Parallax** — Swiss design has no layers; it is systematically flat
Custom cursors
* **Decorative loading animations** — the page loads; it does not perform while loading

WHAT TO STRICTLY AVOID IN SWISS DESIGN

* **More than one accent color** — the system is absolute. One color serves identity and priority marking. Two colors create a hierarchy ambiguity.
* **Centered body text** — flush left is a functional requirement, not a stylistic preference. Centered body text violates the legibility principles the style is built on.
* **Justified body text** — for the same reason: irregular word spacing disrupts reading
* **Decorative typography** — any font other than a neutral grotesque (Helvetica-family) for primary text violates the systematic neutrality
* **Rounded corners** — they communicate approachability, which is a personal expression, which is inadmissible in the International Style
* **Inconsistent grid adherence** — one element that does not sit on the baseline grid disrupts the invisible systematic harmony of the entire composition
* **Gradients or textures** — the surface is flat, white, and neutral. The information organizes itself against this neutral ground.

## Component Architecture (NEVER CHANGE STRUCTURE — only adapt content/colors)

### A. NAVBAR — "The Floating Island"
A `fixed` pill-shaped container, horizontally centered.
- **Morphing Logic:** Transparent with light text at hero top. Transitions to `bg-[background]/60 backdrop-blur-xl` with primary-colored text and a subtle `border` when scrolled past the hero. Use `IntersectionObserver` or ScrollTrigger.
- Contains: Logo (brand name as text), 3-4 nav links, CTA button (accent color).

### B. HERO SECTION — "The Opening Shot"
- `100dvh` height. Full-bleed background image (sourced from Unsplash matching preset's `imageMood`) with a heavy **primary-to-black gradient overlay** (`bg-gradient-to-t`).
- **Layout:** Content pushed to the **bottom-left third** using flex + padding.
- **Typography:** Large scale contrast following the preset's hero line pattern. First part in bold sans heading font. Second part in massive serif italic drama font (3-5x size difference).
- **Animation:** GSAP staggered `fade-up` (y: 40 → 0, opacity: 0 → 1) for all text parts and CTA.
- CTA button below the headline, using the accent color.

### C. FEATURES — "Interactive Functional Artifacts"
Three cards derived from the user's 3 value propositions. These must feel like **functional software micro-UIs**, not static marketing cards. Each card gets one of these interaction patterns:

**Card 1 — "Diagnostic Shuffler":** 3 overlapping cards that cycle vertically using `array.unshift(array.pop())` logic every 3 seconds with a spring-bounce transition (`cubic-bezier(0.34, 1.56, 0.64, 1)`). Labels derived from user's first value prop (generate 3 sub-labels).

**Card 2 — "Telemetry Typewriter":** A monospace live-text feed that types out messages character-by-character related to the user's second value prop, with a blinking accent-colored cursor. Include a "Live Feed" label with a pulsing dot.

**Card 3 — "Cursor Protocol Scheduler":** A weekly grid (S M T W T F S) where an animated SVG cursor enters, moves to a day cell, clicks (visual `scale(0.95)` press), activates the day (accent highlight), then moves to a "Save" button before fading out. Labels from user's third value prop.

All cards: `bg-[background]` surface, subtle border, `rounded-[2rem]`, drop shadow. Each card has a heading (sans bold) and a brief descriptor.

### D. PHILOSOPHY — "The Manifesto"
- Full-width section with the **dark color** as background.
- A parallaxing organic texture image (Unsplash, `imageMood` keywords) at low opacity behind the text.
- **Typography:** Two contrasting statements. Pattern:
  - "Most [industry] focuses on: [common approach]." — neutral, smaller.
  - "We focus on: [differentiated approach]." — massive, drama serif italic, accent-colored keyword.
- **Animation:** GSAP `SplitText`-style reveal (word-by-word or line-by-line fade-up) triggered by ScrollTrigger.

### E. PROTOCOL — "Sticky Stacking Archive"
3 full-screen cards that stack on scroll.
- **Stacking Interaction:** Using GSAP ScrollTrigger with `pin: true`. As a new card scrolls into view, the card underneath scales to `0.9`, blurs to `20px`, and fades to `0.5`.
- **Each card gets a unique canvas/SVG animation:**
  1. A slowly rotating geometric motif (double-helix, concentric circles, or gear teeth).
  2. A scanning horizontal laser-line moving across a grid of dots/cells.
  3. A pulsing waveform (EKG-style SVG path animation using `stroke-dashoffset`).
- Card content: Step number (monospace), title (heading font), 2-line description. Derive from user's brand purpose.

### F. MEMBERSHIP / PRICING
- Three-tier pricing grid. Card names: "Essential", "Performance", "Enterprise" (adjust to fit brand).
- **Middle card pops:** Primary-colored background with an accent CTA button. Slightly larger scale or `ring` border.
- If pricing doesn't apply, convert this into a "Get Started" section with a single large CTA.

### G. FOOTER
- Deep dark-colored background, `rounded-t-[4rem]`.
- Grid layout: Brand name + tagline, navigation columns, legal links.
- **"System Operational" status indicator** with a pulsing green dot and monospace label.

## Technical Requirements (NEVER CHANGE)

- **Stack:** React 19, Tailwind CSS v3.4.17, GSAP 3 (with ScrollTrigger plugin), Lucide React for icons.
- **Fonts:** Load via Google Fonts `<link>` tags in `index.html` based on the selected preset.
- **Images:** Use real Unsplash URLs. Select images matching the preset's `imageMood`. Never use placeholder URLs.
- **File structure:** Single `App.jsx` with components defined in the same file (or split into `components/` if >600 lines). Single `index.css` for Tailwind directives + noise overlay + custom utilities.
- **No placeholders.** Every card, every label, every animation must be fully implemented and functional.
- **Responsive:** Mobile-first. Stack cards vertically on mobile. Reduce hero font sizes. Collapse navbar into a minimal version.
